02

Patterns we keep falling into

Five things the record keeps showing, across more than one window. These are shapes the conversation takes, not charges against either person.

Evidence – what was measured

  • Naomi’s word total runs ahead of Alex’s in every window: 1.83× at Baseline, 1.12× in Conflict, 3.14× in Aftermath.
  • Typical turn length (median words) moves in opposite directions — Naomi 44.5 → 39.5 → 66; Alex 31.5 → 21 → 26. Alex’s longest single turns are 435 and 357 words.
Naomi44.5 → 39.5 → 66Alex31.5 → 21 → 26

Median words per turn — Baseline, Conflict, Aftermath.

Interpretation – one possible reading

It often looks like two different settings running at once: length building on one side, and on the other a typical turn that shrinks by about a third going into the Conflict and never comes back up. Averages hide this — by the mean, Alex’s output looks flat across all three windows, because a handful of very long turns hold the average up over a much shorter floor.

Evidence – what was measured

  • Median reply time — Naomi 484s → ~120s → 169s; Alex 101s → ~94s → 122s.
  • The gap between them closes from 4.8× at Baseline to 1.4× in Aftermath. Alex is faster in every window; his slowest phase is still within 20 seconds of his Baseline.
Naomi484s → 120s → 169sAlex101s → 94s → 122s

Median reply time — Baseline, Conflict, Aftermath. Lower is faster.

Interpretation – one possible reading

The larger movement here is not that one person is faster — that was already true. It’s that roughly eight minutes became roughly two. A change that size is consistent with the channel becoming something checked rather than answered on your own clock. Whatever else the conflict involved, one visible cost is the ability to put the phone down.

Evidence – what was measured

  • In Conflict: 83 questions across 218 turns — 38 per 100 turns, with both people above 35 at the same time. In Aftermath, Naomi’s rate reaches 50.8 per 100 turns, her highest of any window.
  • Applying one consistent timing rule (no reply from the other person within 10 minutes, same session), Conflict questions go unanswered at almost identical rates: Naomi 18%, Alex 16%.

Interpretation – one possible reading

On this measure, neither person is the one who won’t engage. Both are present at high volume and neither is answering. One exchange holds the whole shape: How am I gaslighting me? Tell me how. Explain to me how. met with No, but can you just answer my questions from earlier? — each declining on the grounds that the other declined first.

Evidence – what was measured

  • Sessions opened, Naomi to Alex: 5 : 5 at Baseline, 5 : 5 in Conflict, 5 : 0 in Aftermath. On the count, initiation was exactly even until it wasn’t.
  • The Baseline count needs a qualifier: 4 of Alex’s 5 opens there are one- or two-turn drops that produce no exchange. Only one draws a reply.
  • Explicit verbal sign-offs across all 378 events: Alex 15, Naomi 0 — 11 in Conflict, 4 in Aftermath.

Interpretation – one possible reading

The even 5:5 is accurate and slightly misleading at the same time. Most of the Baseline opens on one side function as broadcast — content put into the channel — rather than as an opening that asks for a reply, so the shared-initiation figure was already carrying less than it looks like it does.

Underneath that, the starting function and the stopping function are the same fact from opposite sides. Effort to keep the channel open sits in one pair of hands; the move that ends a conversation sits in the other. Neither is comfortable to carry, and while it holds, neither person can produce a result alone.

Evidence – what was measured

  • All 105 of 105 Aftermath events are audio. The Conflict window contains 69 text events (38 Alex, 31 Naomi) — both people text heavily — and not one text event survives into July.
  • Naomi’s turns in the two sessions recorded as Naomi-only visibly answer live input: So you didn’t read my messages is what you’re telling me right now, Why are you still responding to me then.

Interpretation – one possible reading

This is the single largest uncertainty in the July analysis. The direction of the July patterns looks solid; the size of them does not. Figures like “opened 5 of 5 sessions” and “52% of questions unanswered” are best read as the outer edge of the asymmetry within the voice channel, not a measure of everything that passed between the two of you.

03

How questions work between us

Five places where the same exchange tends to feel different depending on which end of it you’re standing at. Both descriptions can be accurate at once.

From Naomi’s side

A question is unanswered when no reply arrives at all. In Aftermath that’s 16 of 31 — and the complaint is stated plainly at the time: you literally haven’t answered a single fucking one of my questions.

From Alex’s side

A question is unanswered when a reply arrives that doesn’t address it, or when the asking happened earlier and went nowhere. I’ve asked you, I don’t know how many times tonight alone, what’s going on? — How am I gaslighting me? Tell me how. Explain to me how. I need to have the feedback.

Evidence – what was measured

  • Counted by content judgment, Conflict unanswered runs Alex 15/44 (34%) against Naomi 8/39 (20%). Counted by the 10-minute timing rule, it runs Alex 7/44 (16%) against Naomi 7/39 (18%).

Interpretation – one possible reading

Both people experience themselves as the unanswered one. Naomi because her explicit questions feel unaddressed; Alex because his repeated what’s going on? probes feel ignored in the moment and answered only after he has left. Each definition puts the deficit on the other side, and neither is unreasonable — “you didn’t reply” and “you replied without answering” are both real experiences of not being answered.

From Naomi’s side

The record should be enough on its own. The questions are written in the chat. You can just go have a look. I don’t know why you want me to type them out again. Like, do you want me to do that labor for you?

From Alex’s side

The charges need itemising before they can be answered. What are your questions?, asked more than once — and May I ask you what things I did to make you feel like I was flirting with you more so than anyone else?

Evidence – what was measured

  • The same request recurs across the Conflict window: one person asking for the list to be restated, the other declining to restate it. Naomi also carries the explaining by length — median turn rises to 66 words in Aftermath, with one turn of 928.

Interpretation – one possible reading

The dispute here is over who has to organise the narrative, not only over what happened. One position is that the questions already exist and re-typing them is work that shouldn’t fall to the person who was hurt; the other is that a charge can’t be answered until it’s named specifically. Both are coherent, and between them the structuring labour has nowhere to land.

From Naomi’s side

Stated at the time, on 4 July: when you’re messaging me question marks… it’s hard for me… there’s other stuff going on in my life and it’s hard for me to manage everything.

From Alex’s side

Stated in the same exchange: I now just feel like I’m badgering you and a burden. The asking and the sense of being unwelcome for asking arrive together.

Evidence – what was measured

  • In Conflict, Alex runs 3 question volleys (three or more consecutive question-bearing turns) against Naomi’s 1, plus 3 bare-punctuation turns (??, soooo ??) and 11 turns of three words or fewer.

Interpretation – one possible reading

A bare ?? carries no information — it’s purely a request for a response, which is what makes it land as urgency rather than curiosity. Structurally these moves raise the chance of a fast reply and lower the chance of a considered one. That can be true at the same time as the asking coming from alarm rather than pressure-seeking.

From Naomi’s side

Direct means naming your own feelings and motives. You said, do you want to hang out? You didn’t say, I want to hang out. — you never say hey I want to hang out with you — state your intentions. State what you want.

From Alex’s side

Direct means naming the topic plainly so it can be addressed. How can I acknowledge a conversation that you won’t tell me what it is? — and, of his own earlier messages, I was being very straightforward. Just trying to not sound conceited.

Evidence – what was measured

  • The 4 July exchange goes over the same set of messages twice, each person reading them as evidence of the other’s indirectness. Directness is raised as a complaint by both parties inside the same conversation.

Interpretation – one possible reading

One definition locates directness in stating feelings and motives; the other locates it in specifying topics and questions. Under those two rules the very same message — do you want to hang out? — is either straightforward or evasive, depending on which rule is applied. Each person can be following their own faithfully and still experience the other as dodging.

From Naomi’s side

The repeated asking arrives as load on a night already full. You are badgering me a bit, I’m not gonna lie — it’s hard for me — when you’re messaging me question marks.

From Alex’s side

The same asking is how the checking-in is being done, and it is landing as unwelcome. I’ve been patient. I’ve been fucking respectful… I now just feel like I’m badgering you and a burden.

Evidence – what was measured

  • Both readings attach to the same behaviour: 3 question volleys and 3 bare-punctuation prompts (??, soooo ??) in the Conflict window, and a 4 July sequence where the checking-in and the objection to it sit inside one exchange.
  • The word badgering is used by both people about the same behaviour, minutes apart.

Interpretation – one possible reading

More questions strengthen the “care” reading and the “pressure” reading at the same time, by the same amount. That is what makes this one hard to resolve by asking differently or asking more: the same behaviour can be sincerely offered as attention and sincerely received as load, without either person misreading it.

From Naomi’s side

In July, 2,585 words — 37.7% of everything she says that month — come after Alex has left or in sessions he never joins.

From Alex’s side

In the Conflict window it runs the other way: 843 words (about 16% of his output there) are post-exit or solo tails, against Naomi’s 414 (about 7%).

Evidence – what was measured

  • Conflict tails: Alex 12 turns / 843 words, Naomi 10 turns / 414 words. Aftermath tails: Naomi 11 post-exit turns / 1,792 words plus 8 solo turns / 793 words; Alex zero.

Interpretation – one possible reading

Continuing after the other person has gone isn’t a habit belonging to one of you — it moves between you depending on the window. What changes is which one has more left to say once the exchange has closed. The July tails carry the strongest pursuit reading, and they are also the ones most exposed to the missing-text caveat.

My side of the pattern

The same record, split by person. Each card describes a tendency the data keeps showing, and one reading of what sitting inside it might be like.

Naomi

187 turns across the record

Evidence – what was measured

  • Aftermath: 6,852 words across 61 turns, a 3.14× ratio. Median turn rises from 44.5 words at Baseline to 66; the longest single turn runs 928 words.
  • The change is in length, not frequency — the number of turns per contact day doesn’t rise the way word count does.
Median turn44.5 → 39.5 → 66 words

Median words per turn across the three windows.

Interpretation – one possible reading

Adding length rather than adding messages tends to be the signature of trying to be understood rather than trying to be answered — more evidence, more context, more restatement, set against a sense that the point isn’t landing. There’s a trade-off built into it: the longer a turn is, the more certain it is that everything got said, and the harder it is for any single part to be replied to.

Evidence – what was measured

  • Median reply time falls from 484 seconds at Baseline to about 120 in Conflict and 169 in Aftermath — roughly 3× faster than where it started.
  • The Baseline figure rests on only 7 measured replies, so it describes that window loosely rather than precisely.
Reply484s → 120s → 169s

Median reply time. Lower is faster.

Interpretation – one possible reading

At Baseline the slower pace reads less like low investment and more like not watching the channel — speaking when there was something to say, on her own clock. That’s the one window where that appears true. What replaced it is a much shorter loop, which is consistent with the channel becoming something monitored.

Evidence – what was measured

  • Unanswered rate: 50% at Baseline (6/12), 20% in Conflict, 52% in Aftermath (16/31). Before and after look the same.
  • At Baseline, 5 of the 6 unanswered questions sit in sessions Alex never joined at all — and 3 of 10 Baseline sessions contain only Naomi turns (16 turns, 1,082 words).
Unanswered50% → 20% → 52%

Share of her questions with no reply in the window.

Interpretation – one possible reading

Those two situations feel different even though they count the same. Speaking into an empty room produces “does this reach anyone”; being read and not answered produces “you are choosing not to answer me.” The Baseline pattern looks more like the first. What the record shows shifting over time is the demand itself — moving from resolution towards acknowledgement.

Evidence – what was measured

  • In Aftermath, 2,585 of 6,852 words (37.7%) are produced after Alex has left the session or in sessions he never joined.
  • The record ends this way: on 21 July Alex signs off at 21:20 and 6 further turns / 765 words follow over 28 minutes. Across all 378 events, Naomi issues zero explicit sign-offs.

Interpretation – one possible reading

This is the hardest pattern in the record to read charitably in either direction, and it deserves care. It is consistent with simply not being finished — having something that hasn’t been received and no other route to deliver it. It is equally consistent with the leaving itself being what’s being protested. Her own words at the time point at the second. Worth holding alongside it: if replies were coming by text in July, these turns are one half of a two-sided exchange rather than speech into silence.

188 turns across the record

Evidence – what was measured

  • In Conflict he takes 124 turns to Naomi’s 94 while using fewer words — median turn 21 words, including 11 turns of three words or fewer.
  • The same window holds his longest turn in the record at 435 words (357 in Aftermath). Typical and maximum sit far apart.

Interpretation – one possible reading

In the Conflict window this isn’t withdrawal — it’s the phase where he is the more frequent party. What the pattern looks like is fragmenting rather than retreating: many short arrivals instead of sustained turns. The withdrawal reading belongs to July and doesn’t fit here.

Evidence – what was measured

  • 15 explicit sign-offs across the record — 11 in Conflict, 4 in Aftermath — against zero from Naomi. For example: I’m not going to continue to argue about this tonight, alright? So I’ll speak to you later on.
  • Sessions closed: 6 of 10 at Baseline, 7 of 10 in Conflict.

Interpretation – one possible reading

Read most simply, this is an exit taken instead of an escalation — announcing the ending rather than fading out or going up a level. Each individual exit is defensible on its own terms. The structural side of it is that 15 of them across 13 weeks means no thread in this record ever reaches an ending both people were part of.

Evidence – what was measured

  • Median reply time 101s → ~94s → 122s. July is his slowest window and still faster than Naomi’s 169s, and within 20 seconds of his own Baseline.
  • Words per message, by the mean, stay level across all three windows: 46 / 42 / 50.
Reply101s → 94s → 122s

Median reply time. Lower is faster.

Interpretation – one possible reading

This looks like being present but rationing what gets contributed — not slow, not silent, not unreachable. What changed in July isn’t responsiveness; it’s initiation and duration, which are the two things that would signal the relationship is his to carry.

Evidence – what was measured

  • 21 July, cleanly visible: 25 minutes of easy reciprocal banter and fast replies, then the dinner debt is raised at 21:14 and the session ends at 21:20.
  • He opens 0 of 5 July sessions, against 5 opens in each of the two prior windows. On 13 July he does the opposite — 215 words of substantive acknowledgement, then a disclosure, then an exit at 22:12: I’m getting worked up, so I’m actually going to put my phone on silent.

Interpretation – one possible reading

The 21 July sequence is the July window in miniature: warmth is available, the grievance is not. Whatever that costs on the other side, it’s also a fairly precise statement of a limit. Zero initiation from someone who opened half of all sessions before reads most simply as cost control — answering, but not starting something he expects to have to end.

An alternative worth holding: it is also what politely ending a friendship looks like from the outside. The data cannot tell those two apart.

05

What the data can and can’t say

Four limits that bear directly on how much weight any of the above can hold. They are part of the finding, not footnotes to it.

Evidence – what was measured

  • The reading that Alex’s questions were ignored more (34% against 20%) rests on a content judgment — a reply arrived, but didn’t address the question. Under a purely mechanical timing rule the two converge at 16% and 18%, which is equal within noise.
  • The annotation layer that would let anyone check that judgment is not present in the artifact set.

Interpretation – one possible reading

This one should be held loosely. It is a plausible reading of the transcript, but it cannot be established from timing alone, and it is the weakest-supported claim in the whole analysis.

Evidence – what was measured

  • All 105 July events are audio; the Conflict window contains 69 text events from both people, and none survive into July. That is a filter on the data, not a finding about the month.
  • Naomi’s turns in the sessions recorded as solo respond to live input that isn’t in the record.

Interpretation – one possible reading

The direction of the July findings looks robust; the magnitude is not established. Anything built on “5 to 0” or on the 3.14× volume ratio should be read as the outer edge of the asymmetry within one channel.

Evidence – what was measured

  • 36 Conflict events carry approximate or minute-only timestamps. Depending on how they are handled, Conflict reply medians move — Naomi 112–120s, Alex 70–94s, ratio 1.2× to 1.7×.
  • 77 of 378 events carry no content hash, all of them in the Conflict window, so the usual record-matching rule can’t be applied to the Conflict text thread.

Interpretation – one possible reading

The direction of the Conflict latency finding is stable across every handling of these events. The exact number is soft, and shouldn’t be quoted to the second.

Evidence – what was measured

  • Baseline reply medians rest on 7 and 8 observations; Baseline question counts are 12 and 4, with confidence intervals wide enough (22–78% and 0–67%) that the rates can’t be pinned down.
  • Every emotional label used in this analysis is marked in the source data as interpretation, not a measured quantity.

Interpretation – one possible reading

The Baseline sample is large enough to remove the basis for calling July’s unanswered rate distinctive. It is not large enough to prove the two windows are the same. Both statements are true at once, and the second is often dropped.
